I design numerical methods for PDEs whose solutions are hard to resolve: jumps in material coefficients, sharp interfaces, free boundaries, and tightly coupled physics. My approach is to keep the structure of a classical discretization (finite elements, discontinuous Galerkin, proper orthogonal decomposition, multigrid) and use machine learning or quantum computing only where it does work the classical method cannot. The applications are subsurface energy (geothermal reservoirs, heterogeneous porous media) and glaciology.

Theme 1

Scientific machine learning for PDEs

Neural networks that respect the mathematics of the problem. Each method trains on the residual or energy of a classical formulation (discontinuous Galerkin, POD, or an obstacle problem), so it needs little or no solution data and inherits the structure that makes the classical method reliable near jumps, interfaces, and free boundaries.

1.1Neural operators for non-smooth PDEs

Most neural PDE solvers need large sets of precomputed solutions, and they smooth out jumps. I build operator networks that train on the residual of a finite element discretization, so they need no solution data and inherit the accuracy of the underlying scheme near interfaces.

DG-FEONet: Discontinuous Galerkin Finite Element Operator Network

Accepted SIAM Journal on Scientific Computing, 2026

K. Chawla, Y. Hong, J. Y. Lee, S. Lee

A network maps the PDE input (a discontinuous coefficient or source term) to the coefficients of a discontinuous Galerkin solution. Training minimizes the residual of the symmetric interior penalty (SIPG) weak form, so no input–solution pairs are needed. The paper includes a convergence analysis.

ResultResolves interface jumps that continuous Galerkin and PINN-type solvers smooth over, and generalizes across random circular inclusions with varying radius and contrast.

1.2Reduced-order models for geothermal systems

Coupled thermo-hydro-mechanical (THM) models track displacement, pressure, and temperature in a geothermal reservoir. With heterogeneous rock and fractures, each simulation is costly. Reduced-order models replace the many unknowns with a few coordinates; the question is how to keep accuracy when linear POD needs too many modes.

SparseModesNet for coupled THM systems in heterogeneous porous media

Submitted Computational Mechanics, 2026

K. Chawla, T. Koike, S. Lee, J. Bessac

SparseModesNet starts from a large dictionary of POD modes, learns which small subset to keep (not necessarily the highest-energy ones), and adds a neural correction to the linear POD reconstruction. This is its first use on coupled multiphysics fields.

ResultWith the same 15 reduced coordinates, the full-state relative error drops from 5.8×10⁻² (POD) to 8.3×10⁻⁵, nearly three orders of magnitude. Tested on heterogeneous, fracture-network, and 3D Utah FORGE geometries.

1.3Ice-sheet dynamics as obstacle problems

Ph.D. work with W. Holmes and R. Temam. Ice thickness cannot be negative, and where the ice ends is not known in advance. Writing the shallow ice approximation as an obstacle problem handles both at once, and it gives an energy or residual that a neural network can minimize. I used this to predict ice thickness for real ice sheets.

A physics-informed neural network approach to simulating ice dynamics governed by the shallow ice approximation

Published Int. J. Numerical Analysis and Modeling 23(4), 523–550, 2026

K. Chawla, W. Holmes

Treats time-dependent ice flow as a parabolic obstacle problem and solves it with a physics-informed neural network. Captures moving free boundaries in 1D and 2D, and is applied to the Devon Ice Cap using aerogeophysical data from 2000 and 2018.

Journal arXiv

From obstacle problems to neural insights: feed-forward neural network modeling of ice thickness

Published Int. J. Numerical Analysis and Modeling 22, 1–20, 2025

K. Chawla, W. Holmes, R. Temam

Frames the stationary ice-thickness obstacle problem as energy minimization and solves it with a feed-forward network. Tested in 1D and 2D, then applied to the Greenland ice sheet (NSIDC-0092), with pre-training on bedrock topography improving accuracy.

Theme 2

Quantum computing for PDE solvers

Quantum linear solvers promise speedups for the large sparse systems that PDEs produce, but today's devices have a few noisy qubits and return a quantum state, not numbers. My work asks a concrete question: what can a small, noisy quantum computation contribute to a real finite element solve now? I design hybrid algorithms where the quantum device handles one small, well-posed piece of a classical solver, and I measure its effect honestly, counting every shot needed to read the answer out. The test bed is subsurface flow through heterogeneous rock.

2.1Hybrid quantum-classical coarse-to-fine solver

A hybrid quantum-classical coarse-to-fine finite element solver for heterogeneous Darcy flow

Under revision SIAM Journal on Scientific Computing, special section on quantum numerical algorithms, 2026

K. Chawla, S. Lee, J. Valyou

Instead of putting the whole fine-grid system on a quantum device, the quantum computer solves only the small Galerkin coarse problem with a variational method inspired by VQLS. The coarse solution is measured, reconstructed, and prolongated to the fine grid, where it warm-starts a classical algebraic-multigrid preconditioned CG solve that restores full accuracy.

4,095 → 4fine-grid unknowns handed to a 4-qubit register through a 15-unknown Galerkin coarse system
30–35%lower initial L² error than a zero start, from measured quantum solutions under shot and device noise
≈89%fewer shots in time-dependent runs by reusing circuit parameters between steps (210M → 25M per run)
10⁻¹¹relative error after classical correction, even when state fidelity drops to 0.61 under device noise

Why it worksThe coarse quantum answer only needs to capture the large-scale pressure trend; the classical correction supplies the fine detail. Galerkin orthogonality splits the initial error into coarse-space error and error from the quantum solve and readout, so each source can be measured.

What the paper establishes

  • An end-to-end pipeline from a finite element system to a quantum circuit and back to explicit solution coefficients, including tomography and rescaling.
  • A fair comparison of measured quantum, zero, and exact Galerkin starting guesses, using initial L² and energy errors and solver iterations.
  • Steady and time-dependent heterogeneous Darcy flow under finite sampling, synthetic noise, and noise calibrated from an IBM device model.
  • Parameter reuse across time steps: 56–57 of 60 coarse solves needed no further optimization.

NextLarger coarse systems, other coarse spaces, cheaper readout than full tomography, runs on quantum hardware, and other symmetric positive definite systems from multiphysics models.

2.2Mesh-free models of heterogeneous media

The solvers above all need permeability on whatever mesh they use, but the data usually comes as one value per cell of a particular grid. Moving it to a new mesh blurs sharp layers. I build continuous, closed-form surrogates that keep the interfaces and can be evaluated on any mesh, from a coarse quantum-sized grid to a fine one.

PAM: a parallel and adaptive mesh-free method for discontinuous coefficient fields

Submitted Journal of Computational Physics, 2026

K. Chawla, S. Lee, Y. Shin

PAM writes the permeability as a sum of Shepard-normalized radial basis functions. Sparse regression picks the basis functions that matter, and residual-driven refinement adds centers only where the field jumps. Subdomains are fitted in parallel. The paper proves the normalized RBF approximation reaches arbitrarily small L¹ error for step functions.

ResultThree adaptive rounds cut the L² error from 1.55×10⁻³ to 5.6×10⁻⁵. The same surrogate drives Darcy solves on refined, irregular, and perforated meshes, including the SPE10 benchmark.
